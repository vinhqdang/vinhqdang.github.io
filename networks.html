Networks and the Internet

A network is nodes sending data to each other. Build one, break it, zoom out from a home network to the whole Internet, follow a message hop by hop, and see how fast it all grew.

This page supports British University Vietnam students in the module Introduction to Computing Science (2024–2027 and onwards). It accompanies the Week 8 “Networks” lecture. The next lesson, Network protocols, shows how the data actually travels.

1 · What is a network?

Nodes and arcs: a network is a graph

Networks consist of nodes (N) and arcs (A). Writing a network as a graph lets a computer analyse it: if we start at N3, can we get to N2? The same maths describes rail networks, flights and social networks. Redundancy matters too: failures should not bring down the whole system.

4 · The biggest network

        
The Internet: how it grew, and who is on it

        

          
In the beginning

            
A US military communications network of the late 1960s and 70s, designed to be robust even against attack. ARPANET used packet switching rather than an operator connecting wires, with distributed control. Its first four nodes used different protocols; only when people moved jobs between universities did the machines really learn to talk.

          
The Internet is not “the web”

            
The World Wide Web is just one service that runs on the Internet. Email, file transfer, online games and video calls use the same network but different protocols.

          
Why so fast?

            
Accessibility: cheap devices, batteries, Wi-Fi to share one connection. Need: social media, business competition, taxes, gaming and media, shopping, education.

        

        
Hosts on the Internet (log scale)

        

        
Each step up is ten times more hosts. From 111 hosts in 1977 to a billion in 2014: growth of about 9,000,000×. Values from the lecture (2001 is quoted as 150–175 million).


        
The “world-wide” web? Who is connected

        
Internet users as a share of each region’s population (Sept 2020 estimates in the lecture slide). Access is far from equal.

        

        
Access inequality has more causes than infrastructure: cost, language, and rules such as national filtering (for example the “Great Firewall of China”) change what people can reach.
